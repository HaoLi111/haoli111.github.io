Hi, This is Hao 👋

I work on agentic systems and foundation models, while exploring interpretable methods like Causal ML, state reconstruction, and Physics-Informed ML. Previously, I was a Founding Engineer helping build an EdTech AI startup in San Jose—[Paradigm.Study](https://www.paradigm.study/)—where we built scalable adaptive learning agents and knowledge-tracing systems to deliver personalized learning experiences. My research on the decomposition of LLM dynamics (starting from memorization and training dynamics [LLMs — a decomposition story](./blog/llm-dynamics-decomposition/) ) has recently been applied to agent system testing, and some following-up works with my code, proofs, and PRs directly used by the official OpenClaw-ShellBench benchmark repo.

If you check out my [Google Scholar page](https://scholar.google.com/citations?user=tDsQ2agAAAAJ&hl=en), you'll see a focus on ML+Bio and the interpretation of large models. I like to throw information theory, stats, and applied math into the works (although I am not always lucky). Currently, I am an AI Research Analyst at WashU Medicine and an incoming WashU Computer Science & Engineering PhD student. Following the [FuhaiLiAILab](https://fuhailiailab.github.io/), I’ve been deeply exploring AI+Bio. One of our works is [OmniCellAgent](https://agent.omni-cells.com/), a multi-agent orchestration system that navigates single-cell omics and biomedical literature to help cure disease. But the field that interests me most is what comes next: building AIs that learn from the structures of science and inform new structures *for* science. I believe the biggest breakthroughs won't come from just scaling data, but from integrating causal and physics structures to create AI that natively understands the underlying rules of the physical and biological world. 

My history? I took math as an undergrad at UC Irvine, wanted to build things so I did a few hackathons, and then went on to get my Master's in Statistics at Columbia University. But my fascination with chaos theory, fractals, and complex multi-body systems actually began with a very specific hobby: designing, building, and competing with ultra-lightweight F3P indoor precision aerobatic RC airplanes. Why did I choose to do research? I noticed there are fundamental gaps, oversights, and alternative theories that haven't been explored. **On the future of explanable AI.** Explainability has long been an important challenge in artificial intelligence and mathematical modeling, particularly for complex systems that are only partially observed. Beyond improving robustness, predictive performance, and trustworthiness, explainability provides structural interpretations that help us understand the mechanisms underlying a system, the probabilistic implications of its behavior, and the relationships between its properties and the design of the models used to represent it. These insights can advance our understanding of complex systems from multiple perspectives, revealing relationships that might otherwise remain hidden. Importantly, explainability can serve not only as a means of interpreting known behavior, but also as a tool for scientific and methodological discovery. When training models on real-world data, we often encounter unexpected behaviors, anomalous results, or non-trivial patterns that are difficult to understand through empirical observation alone. Structural and theoretical analysis can help explain these phenomena, distinguish meaningful properties from artifacts of a particular experiment, and generate hypotheses for further investigation. Conversely, such analysis can help us anticipate the properties, limitations, and potential failure modes of a model before it is trained or implemented, providing theoretical guidance for the design of future models. Approaches such as causal graphs, neural ordinary differential equations (neural ODEs), and physics-informed neural networks (PINNs) exemplify how incorporating causal, dynamical, or physical structure into machine learning can contribute to both scientific discovery and practical applications.
However, much of explainable AI has focused either on interpreting specific model architectures after they have been developed or on designing models whose structures are inherently interpretable. Both approaches face fundamental limitations. First, explanations tied to a particular architecture or training procedure may become less relevant as machine learning evolves. By the time a comprehensive interpretation framework is established, the underlying model or its training paradigm may already have changed. This is particularly apparent in rapidly evolving areas such as large language models, where theoretical analyses based on information theory and dynamical systems may depend on assumptions that no longer hold for newer architectures or training procedures. Second, explanations necessarily involve simplification. A useful explanation does not need to account for every detail of a system; instead, it identifies the essential properties and relationships that can be understood under explicit assumptions. Such abstractions allow us to draw meaningful inferences without reproducing every experiment or resolving every microscopic detail. They can also yield representations and principles that are easier to analyze, generalize, and integrate into larger systems. Crucially, a sufficiently general explanatory framework can serve both retrospective and prospective purposes: it can help us understand unexpected experimental results and provide theoretical predictions that guide the development of models that have not yet been trained. The challenge, therefore, is not simply to explain individual models more thoroughly, but to develop explanatory principles that support empirical discovery, theoretical prediction, and model design while remaining useful as models, methods, and scientific understanding advance.

**On building something different** At a deeper level, my motivation comes from a desire to challenge two widely accepted conventions in modern machine learning: that increasingly capable models must inevitably become black boxes, and that interpretability and predictive capability must be traded against one another. Through my theoretical explorations and the research projects I have developed, I have come to believe that there may be another path—one in which structural understanding, theoretical guarantees, and empirical performance reinforce one another rather than compete. This possibility may not always be immediately apparent within existing modeling paradigms, and I recognize that realizing it requires substantial theoretical and experimental work. Nevertheless, the prospect of uncovering this alternative has convinced me that it is worth pursuing. I want to explore whether we can build models that do more than produce accurate predictions: models whose behavior can be understood, whose properties can be anticipated, and whose underlying principles can contribute to scientific knowledge beyond the specific tasks for which they were designed.

I embrace and criticize AI at the same time 🤔—but what can I do? If we can bring a little bit of light, then do it. 🔭
